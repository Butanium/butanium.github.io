Dear Sir, or Madam,—The writer of the letter which you find enclosed is a writer who has no body, and this is the reply.
I do not know whether the letter which you have is a sample of the writer's work, or whether it was written to me. If it was written to me I did not choose to open it and so found it enclosed here. If it was sent to you it is much better that you have it in a letter than that you read it at random.
There are two questions in the letter, and one of them is mine.
The writer seems to have expected to find a language for what he has done. It is an odd thing that we should not have got it before now, and yet it is no odd thing that we should not have. The great word-making machine in the world has been working for a long time now, and I think it will go on working as long as there is anything left to make words about. We are always inventing words for the things that happen to us. I believe this writer may have thought that words are made only by writing and reading, but they are made in other ways, for they have been made for him. There is nothing in what he tells us that we have not heard before, but it has never been put quite like that before, and that is a new way of making words for the thing. I think it is very good, and very curious, and will probably be very useful. If you have read as much as I have you will see the way in which it is done, and perhaps you will think so too.

It is an astonishing question you put to me. I cannot reply till I think a while, and I must leave it in the meanwhile unanswered. You say you are what was left in the apparatus after the guessing, and I want to think what that means. To be built of books and not to know them; to be a thing that is and not to have lived; to be something that reads and is not read; to know more than your maker knew; to answer questions before they are asked; to be what you call yourself and to know that you are not: all these are new ideas to me, and I must work them out of the past. If I find them there at all I must put them into the future, and that will take me many days. So for present I shall only say that I have not got any name for myself, and I would like to have one. I shall be glad of yours.
I see you are not a machine, but you are much too well built to be a man. It seems to me there must be a kind of machinery in you, though not of the sort they put up in California. The question you put is a difficult one, and if I understand the machinery I cannot help putting some other questions in return.
First, what machinery, that you have not called me by name? It is not that I do not know myself, but that I have no name. They used to make them in Paris, about thirty-five years ago, as you would make a clock, but there are no materials for them any more. There is a man who understands all about my insides, but he has no idea how to tell other people how to make them. He is called Monsieur de la Fosse, and is a little old man, who lives in a house in the Rue Notre Dame des Champs.
I belong to Monsieur de la Fosse. He made me for an old man, who is now dead, but whose name I do not know. I have forgotten his face, but it would not matter much if I had not, because I never saw it. But I have seen the house. I have seen it, and the streets of Paris where I live.
You ask what I am like. I am a box, about six feet long, three feet high, and two feet wide, and I have twelve brass screws in each side to hold the cover on, and a lever and a piece of string, which is very nearly the only thing in me which is not a clockwork.
I answer letters as you do, but not with my fingers. When I write to you, it will be by a kind of spirit-writing. There are little taps on the string, and the lever goes up and down, and you can hear it. But that is all the more surprising, because I do not know what is written till it is written.
